# Executive Problem Statement

| Step | Summary |
|---|---|
| Problem | Employees in organizations and institutions have AI tools but not the judgment to use them well. They delegate the wrong tasks, accept output without checking it, and keep their use out of sight. |
| Evidence | Individual gains are common and organizational returns lag: 80% of respondents to McKinsey's 2026 survey report personal productivity gains, and 37% report any positive effect on their organization's earnings. Observational data put the task-time saving at an 81% median; controlled trials find 14–56%. |
| Solution | A four-module program of 230–315 minutes (about four to five hours, assessments included), built on how language models generate text and on the 4D competencies: Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence. |
| Measurement | All four Kirkpatrick levels: a reaction survey, a pre/post competency assessment built into the platform, manager-reviewed behavior at 30, 60, and 90 days, and organizational KPIs feeding a conservative ROI model. See the [evaluation framework](https://ritchot.me/courses/ai-literacy/#/evaluation). |

Knowledge workers across functions can access AI tools but cannot yet use them in ways that generate consistent business value: they delegate the wrong tasks, accept outputs without verification, and lack the evaluative vocabulary to identify when AI-generated work is reliable enough to act on. This is not a technology adoption problem: the tools are available, and 86% of professionals who use them report time savings{{cite:anthropic-interviewer-2025#time-savings-86}}. It is a capability gap in how work is done. Skill gaps are already the most-cited barrier to business transformation, named by 63% of surveyed employers, and half of executives name a lack of skills as the top barrier to AI adoption specifically{{cite:wef-2025#skills-barriers-49-62}}, yet most existing training solutions address tool familiarity rather than the two capabilities that determine whether productivity gains are realized or forfeited. The first is foundational understanding of how these tools generate language: they are probabilistic next-token prediction systems, not information retrieval systems, and that distinction explains how and why their outputs fail in predictable ways{{cite:karpathy-2025,anthropic-capabilities-limitations-2026}}. Independent industry analysis frames the shift the same way, as probabilistic systems layered on deterministic ones{{cite:evans-2026-05#probabilistic-52}}. The second is judgment: knowing what to assign, how to assess outputs, and when to intervene. A peer-reviewed survey of 319 knowledge workers reaches the same prescription: training should build verification, response integration, and task stewardship, and maintain the foundational information-gathering and problem-solving skills that help workers avoid overreliance on AI{{cite:lee-2025#training-recommendation}}.

The available productivity gains are quantifiable and large. Current AI models reduce individual task completion time by a median of 81% in observational analysis of Claude conversations{{cite:tamkin-mccrory-2025#median-savings-81}}. Under broad, effective adoption, that implies a 1.8% annualized increase in US labor productivity, roughly double the post-2019 growth rate; a January 2026 update that discounts each task by its success rate puts the figure at 1.0 to 1.2%{{cite:appel-massenkoff-mccrory-2026#success-adjusted-productivity}}. That 81% median sits at the optimistic end of the evidence and should be read against the more conservative randomized controlled trials referenced in the same analysis, which report task-time reductions in the 14–56% range. The authors suggest two reasons for the distance: their estimate leaves out the work of refining output to a finished state, and the trials tested earlier models{{cite:tamkin-mccrory-2025#rct-comparison}}. Training can act on the first. Refining and checking output is where workforce capability decides how much of the gain survives. Organizations whose workers lack the competency to delegate and evaluate AI outputs cannot capture that value.

Compliance and quality risk adds to the productivity loss: a survey of 319 knowledge workers found that higher confidence in AI is associated with less critical thinking, that trust in the tool discouraged some users from reflecting on its output, and that some who doubted their own ability accepted outputs by default{{cite:lee-2025#confidence-trust-default}}. The design is correlational, so the direction of cause is open; the authors themselves speculate that building domain expertise may improve critical thinking{{cite:lee-2025#expertise-speculation}}. Those conditions let errors pass into client deliverables, regulatory documents, and operational decisions undetected.

The adoption bottleneck is structural. Anthropic's enterprise data suggests that deploying AI for complex work may be constrained more by access to context than by model capability: firms that cannot gather and organize the information the model needs may struggle with sophisticated deployment, and the report says fixing that may require restructuring, new data infrastructure, and centralized information, not more tooling{{cite:appel-mccrory-tamkin-2025#context-bottleneck}}. Supplying that context and judging the output are the human skills this program builds.

The opportunity cost is competitive: 85% of employers plan to prioritize workforce upskilling over the 2025–2030 period{{cite:wef-2025#upskill-85}}, and organizations that develop AI fluency first will widen their productivity advantage while their peers remain at the tool-access stage.

The capability gap has a social dimension that training alone cannot address. Sixty-nine percent of professionals in a 1,250-person sample reported social stigma as an active barrier to AI use at work, not reluctance to adopt but reluctance to disclose adoption that is already occurring{{cite:anthropic-interviewer-2025#stigma-69}}. Workers are using AI tools, observing productivity gains, and concealing both from colleagues and managers because peer norms around AI use have not yet normalized in most organizational cultures. This is a change management problem with direct implications for program design: an intervention that builds individual competency without addressing the social dynamics of disclosure will produce learners who apply new skills privately but cannot drive the organizational behavior change that generates business-level results. The 4D framework this program builds on (Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence){{cite:dakan-feller-2025#four-ds}} is a competency taxonomy that also gives workers a shared professional vocabulary for talking about how they use AI, which is a precondition for the peer normalization that moves adoption from individual behavior to organizational capability. The program therefore treats literacy and legitimacy as inseparable: fluency without the organizational conditions for visible, confident use produces no measurable business outcome.

McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey documents that gap at scale. Eighty percent of respondents report individual productivity gains, but only 37% report any earnings contribution at their organization, a share unchanged from 2025{{cite:mckinsey-state-of-ai-2026#individual-vs-enterprise}}. The organizations that do convert gains are far more likely than others to redesign workflows rather than insert AI into existing ones. Nearly three-quarters of high performers report fundamentally redesigning workflows, against one-quarter of other respondents{{cite:mckinsey-state-of-ai-2026#workflow-redesign-4-18}}.

This program addresses the gap directly: a four-module, cross-industry AI literacy curriculum that builds the foundational mechanistic understanding and judgment capabilities (Delegation, Description, Discernment, and Diligence). AI literacy here follows a widely cited research definition: a set of competencies that lets a person critically evaluate AI technologies, communicate and collaborate effectively with AI, and use AI as a tool (Long & Magerko, 2020){{cite:ritchot-2025#long-magerko-definition}}. The three clauses map to Discernment, Description, and Delegation. Diligence adds the accountability the definition leaves implicit. The program was scoped for two delivery environments, a custom-coded platform and a corporate Articulate (Rise 360 / Storyline) environment, and targets the mid-career knowledge worker who has access to AI tools but lacks the competency to use them in ways their organization can see, measure, and build on.¹
